Crystal morphology for petrochronology & thermochronology

Mineral Habit Matrix

These are idealized convex crystal habits. Each one is built from the mineral's lattice parameters, Miller-index face forms and the relative development of each form. Zircon follows the Pupin (1980) typology grid. The other minerals are laid out on habit matrices taken from the morphology and grain-geometry literature. Drag any crystal to rotate the whole grid, and click one to inspect it and download STL or STEP files.

      How the geometries are built

      1. Lattice to face normals. Each face normal comes from the reciprocal lattice, nhkl = h a* + k b* + l c*. The real cell geometry therefore sets the angles between faces, for example the zircon {101}∧{211} angles from c/a, or the titanite wedge from β. Hexagonal minerals use Miller–Bravais (hkil) indices.
      2. Point-group symmetry. Each form {hkl} is expanded to all of its symmetry-equivalent faces with the mineral's point group (4/mmm, 6/m, m3̄m, 2/m, …). Every face of a form sits at the same central distance from the crystal centre.
      3. Half-space intersection. The crystal is the convex polyhedron n·x ≤ d over all faces, which is the Gibbs–Wulff construction. A face whose plane misses the solid is absent from the habit.
      4. Relative development to distances. Typologies describe how strongly each face is developed, not the face distances themselves. A prism ratio such as {100}:{110} is converted to the fraction of the cross-section perimeter taken by each form. A termination ratio such as {101}:{211} is converted to the fraction of termination face area. Each distance is solved by bisection. Elongation is a rigid translation of the two terminations along the c-axis, so it does not change the termination shape.
      5. Normalization and export. Every crystal is scaled to unit volume (1 mm³) because grain size is outside the scope of this site. Each crystal is written as a binary STL mesh and as an exact planar B-rep STEP file (AP214) whose faces are labelled with their Miller indices.

      These shapes are idealized. Real grains have rounded edges, are broken or abraded, carry growth zoning and are often mounted in random orientations. The habit matrices sample morphology space as it is described in the cited studies. They are not a fit to any particular sample.
